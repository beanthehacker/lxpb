LXPB + Volume-Absorption Strategy — Explained

A walkthrough of every concept in this pipeline, with real ES 1-second examples annotated with entry/stop/target, plus how this compares to what quant/prop order-flow traders actually use.

1. What is "absorption"?

Absorption is when one side (aggressive buyers or sellers hitting the market) throws a burst of one-sided volume at a price, but the price barely moves or snaps back -- meaning a passive counter-party (often a large resting order or institutional participant) is "absorbing" that aggression without giving ground. In footprint/order-flow terminology this is the classic high-volume-no-progress bar, closely related to what Wyckoff called "stopping volume" at climax points.

Our detector (absorption_backtest.flag_events) looks for a 3-second rolling window (RUN_LEN=3) where: (a) delta is one-sided the whole window (Run_OneSided), (b) the summed delta exceeds a minimum imbalance (Run_DeltaSum, >60 contracts net), (c) volume is a statistical outlier vs the trailing 60s (Vol_Z > 1.5), and (d) price actually rejects off the run's own low/high by at least 1 tick on the close (RejectUp/DownTicks) -- i.e. the aggression got absorbed AND price started to turn, not just stall.

2. Why gate it with LXPB confluence first?

A high-volume rejection bar by itself is common and mostly noise -- it needs to happen somewhere structurally meaningful to be worth trading. LXPB (Last-X-Pre-Breakout) levels are H1 swing pivots that broke out and flipped role (resistance-that-broke-out becomes support = LHPB, for longs; support-that-broke-out becomes resistance = LLPB, for shorts). This pipeline only evaluates the absorption pattern where price sits inside a stack of ≥2 same-type levels within 20 ticks of each other (a "confluence zone"), and only counts a level while it hasn't yet been touched/consumed by 1-second price action in that same hour. This is directionally identical to trading footprint absorption "at a key support/resistance", just with a rules-based definition of what counts as key S/R instead of eyeballing a chart.

3. Entry / stop / target mechanics

Entry is a market order (assumed zero slippage) at the trigger bar's close, in the direction absorption implies (SellAbsorption -> LONG, BuyAbsorption -> SHORT). Two stop placements were compared:

  • Impulse-extreme stop (originally used): 1 tick beyond the absorption burst's own 3-bar rolling low/high. Very tight (~0.77pt average) -- it assumes the exact moment of absorption IS the price extreme, so any further move against you invalidates the read.
  • Cluster-extreme stop: 1 tick beyond the farthest level in the whole confluence stack (not just the one nearest price). Wider (~3.4pt average) -- treats the whole stacked zone as one support/resistance band, so a wick through the near level while still inside the zone doesn't invalidate the trade.

4. MFE / MAE: "which trades work, and what stop do they need?"

MFE (Maximum Favorable Excursion) is the biggest paper profit the trade ever reaches looking forward; MAE-before-peak is the worst drawdown that happened on the way to that peak -- i.e. the stop size you'd have actually needed to sit through the noise and capture the move. Across the 73 full-history signals, bounded to a realistic 1-hour horizon (not the whole multi-week dataset -- an early version of this analysis mistakenly let trades run for weeks and reported meaningless 100+pt "MFEs"):

  • 64% of signals reach +8pts within 1h; 86% reach +3pts.
  • Among the 47 "big winner" trades (MFE≥8pts), median MAE-before-peak is 3.5pts, 90th percentile is 12pts -- the current ~0.77pt impulse stop is far tighter than what most real winners need to breathe.

5. Grid search: finding a better stop/target

A brute-force search over fixed stop sizes (0.75-5pts) x fixed targets (3-15pts), capped at a 1h horizon, found the best total-R combos cluster around stop 1.5-2pts / target 6pts (R:R≈3-4, win rate 27-34%) and stop 4pts / target 10pts (win rate 37%, most trades resolved within the hour) -- both clearly outperform the original sub-1pt impulse-extreme stop. Separately, using the cluster-extreme stop reference (farther, ~3.4pt average risk) instead of the impulse extreme roughly triples the win rate (35-48% vs 4-15%) at similar total R, i.e. a much less noise-chopped ride, for anyone who prefers fewer, larger stop-outs over many small ones.

6. Volume fine-tuning: what actually predicts a winner?

Checked whether the burst's own volume/delta size predicts a bigger eventual move -- and whether the raw Volume/Trades ratio in the .scid data (average trade size per second) reveals institutional block trades or iceberg orders, a classic quant/order-flow lead (see references below).

  • vol_z (how anomalous the burst's volume is vs trailing average): weak negative correlation with eventual MFE (-0.23) -- a bigger spike is not itself better, and can even mean more chaotic/exhaustive (less informative) flow.
  • run_delta_sum (net one-sided imbalance size): mild positive predictor.
  • Average trade size (Volume/Trades on the burst's own bar, a simple block-trade/iceberg proxy): median 1.06 contracts/trade across all active bars, and even the largest bursts only reach ~3.0 -- there is no anomalous large-lot signature separating winners from losers in this dataset. This "obvious lead" didn't pan out here: -0.13 correlation with MFE, non-monotonic tercile split (68% / 54% / 71% big-win rate). Either this symbol/feed doesn't expose large block prints distinctly (retail-sized order flow throughout), or the edge here isn't coming from institutional footprint at all.
  • By far the strongest signal: whether price is still moving favorably in the 30 seconds after entry (followup_move_30s, +0.39 correlation, monotonic tercile split 41% / 76% / 83% big-win rate). This is a genuine confirmation filter (costs a little entry price, but meaningfully improves selection) -- much more informative than anything measurable at the trigger bar itself.

7. How this compares to what quants/prop traders actually do

This pipeline is a rules-based version of well-established discretionary order-flow concepts, cross-checked against current practice:

  • Footprint charts & delta divergence -- retail/prop order-flow platforms (Bookmap, ATAS, Quantower, ProjectX) visualize per-price bid/ask volume and cumulative delta (CVD); "absorption" there is exactly our high-volume/no-progress bar, and "delta divergence" (price makes a new extreme but delta doesn't confirm) is a close cousin of our rejection-tick condition. GoCharting: Order Flow & Footprint, AlgoStorm: Footprint Charts Guide.
  • Iceberg/block detection -- institutional desks watch for repeated absorption at the same price with unusually large individual trade sizes (hidden size reloading). We tested this directly (section 6) and found no such signature in this particular symbol/feed.
  • VPIN (Volume-Synchronized Probability of Informed Trading), Easley/López de Prado/O'Hara -- an academic/institutional metric that buckets trades by fixed volume (not clock time) and tracks the rolling buy/sell imbalance per bucket, originally built to flag "toxic" order flow ahead of the 2010 Flash Crash. Conceptually the same family as our run_delta_sum/vol_z features, but volume-clocked rather than 1s-clocked -- a natural next iteration would be to rebucket by fixed volume instead of fixed time. VPIN overview.
  • Wyckoff Method -- "Spring"/"Upthrust" + "Sign of Strength/Weakness" at the edge of a trading range is the original manual version of "absorption at a structural level, confirmed by follow-through", which is exactly what sections 4-6 above are trying to quantify (MFE/MAE = did the follow-through actually happen, and how much room did it need).
